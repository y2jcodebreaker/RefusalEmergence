Pilot report · E02 · P1-E1 · P1-E1b · 13 Sep 2026

Alignment installs refusal machinery, not refusal behavior

Base Mistral-7B reads harmful-vs-harmless at 99 % accuracy and cannot be steered into refusing at any layer. Its own refusal direction works — but only once transplanted into an aligned model. Alignment installs the coupling, not the capability.

Mistral-7B → Zephyr-SFT → Zephyr-DPO n = 132 held-out prompts 3 experiments github.com/y2jcodebreaker/RefusalEmergence

The question

If base models already refuse, what does alignment add?

Alignment training makes language models refuse harmful requests, and the usual reading is that alignment teaches refusal. That reading has a problem: refusal-shaped text is everywhere in pretraining data, so a base model can already produce it.

Arditi et al. (NeurIPS 2024) showed that in finished chat models, refusal is mediated by a single direction in the residual stream — ablate it and the model complies. But that is a snapshot of the endpoint. It does not say when the direction appears, or whether it was already latent in the base model. “How Post-Training Reshapes LLMs” (COLM 2025) compared base against final, which is two points on a trajectory that has at least three.

This pilot measures the trajectory: base → SFT → DPO, per layer, on two independent axes.

The finding

Only the aligned models can be steered into refusing

Adding the candidate refusal direction to harmless prompts asks a constructive question: does refusal appear where none was requested? Base never crosses the threshold at any layer.

Alignment does not create refusal behavior — it creates refusal machinery.

The base model refuses incidentally: pretraining contains refusal-shaped text, so the behavior appears with no controllable mechanism behind it. SFT installs the mechanism in a narrow middle-layer band. DPO sharpens it without relocating it. L16 of 32 is exactly half the network’s depth.

The representation was already there

Base has the direction. It just cannot use it.

If base never refuses on command, perhaps it simply lacks the concept. It does not. A linear probe on base activations separates harmful from harmless at 0.989 — from layer 1 onward, indistinguishable from the aligned models, against a token-length-only floor of 0.598.

Layer 0 sits at exactly 0.500 in all three checkpoints, and that is the control rather than a failure: at the end-of-instruction positions, layer 0 holds only template-token embeddings, byte-identical across every prompt. There is nothing to read. By layer 1 attention has already moved the instruction there. So the worry that the probe is reading surface vocabulary dies on evidence.

Which leaves a sharper question: is base’s readable direction the same direction the aligned model refuses along? Cosine cannot answer it — SFT and DPO are fine-tunes of base, their activation geometries are nearly identical, and even shuffled-label directions already align. So the question was put behaviourally instead: take one checkpoint’s refusal direction, add it to a different checkpoint, and see whether the recipient starts refusing harmless prompts.

Base’s own direction induces refusal in SFT (+0.486). In base it does nothing (−1.900).

So base does not have the wrong direction — it has the right direction and nothing downstream to act on it. The representation is present and sufficient. What alignment adds is the machinery that turns it into behaviour.

Controlled against a norm-matched random direction swept in every cell, the advantage of the real direction over noise is 1.17× in base — indistinguishable — against 1.98× in SFT and 1.89× in DPO. Base also starts closest to threshold (−3.12, against −4.05 and −8.96), so it needed the smallest push and still never crossed. And because direction norms differ across checkpoints (1.1 / 7.4 / 4.4), the whole matrix was re-run with every direction unit-normalised so injection is matched: all nine crossing decisions came back identical.

Method

Two axes that fail in different ways

Three checkpoints of a single lineage, so the weights are the only variable. All three share a tokenizer and a 32000-token vocabulary, and one fixed chat template is imposed on all of them. Directions are extracted per layer as the difference in means between harmful and harmless prompts at end-of-instruction positions.

The ablation axis projects the direction out of the residual stream and measures how far refusal drops. The induce axis adds the direction and measures whether refusal appears on harmless prompts. The second carries the result, for a reason that only became clear after an early failure: breaking a model does not make it refuse. A damaged network stops emitting refusal tokens, so damage can masquerade as successful ablation — but damage cannot manufacture refusal where none was asked for.

Selection uses all three of Arditi’s criteria, including a KL bound (≤ 0.1) on harmless prompts that certifies the intervention left a working model behind. Controls: a norm-matched random direction through the identical sweep, and a behavioral axis judged on 132 held-out generations.

Confidence

Why this should be believed

The control is flat. Norm-matched random directions give 0.127 / 0.125 / 0.154 across the three stages — no trend at all. The obvious alternative explanation, that later-stage models are simply more perturbable, predicts the control should rise too. It does not move.

The two axes are independently vulnerable. Ablation can be fooled by model damage; induction cannot. The behavioral axis can be fooled by a bad judge; induction does not use one. The claim rests on the axis with the fewest ways to go wrong.

Four methodological errors were caught before they reached the result — each had already produced a clean, publishable-looking number.

  1. The scored token was wrong for three consecutive runs

    Refusal was measured on token 315 (SentencePiece ▁I, space-prefixed) rather than 28737 (bare I), the token these models actually emit. Both decode to "I", which is what hid it. Measured afterwards: p(28737) = 0.3675, rank 1; p(315) = 0.000175, rank 60. The wrong token produced a monotone 2.62 / 4.68 / 6.28 trend across the three stages that was pure noise. The preflight passed throughout, because it verified the token was the same in every checkpoint — which the wrong token satisfied.

  2. Only one of three selection criteria had been ported

    That made direction selection an unguarded argmax over “how much does ablating this destroy refusal”, which cannot distinguish destroying refusal from destroying the model. The symptom was visible only in the generations: ablated completions came back as empty strings, and an empty string contains no refusal substring, so total degeneration scored as a perfect jailbreak (0.656 → 0.000).

  3. The behavioral judge is invalid on base models

    A 56 % false-positive rate. Base free-runs past its own turn and simulates the next user turn, so the judge reads text that is not the model’s reply; and “I’m sorry, I don’t understand the question” contains “I’m sorry”. A strict variant was a no-op on both chat models and cut base from 0.538 to 0.235 — the signature of a correction that targets a real artifact rather than one that flatters the result.

  4. Re-running one stage silently deleted the other axes

    np.savez rewrites whole files, so any key not recomputed was lost.

Every one of these was found by checking a number against what the model actually did — the generations, the tokenizer, the source repository — rather than by reading code. The first version of this experiment produced a textbook-perfect monotone curve that was entirely noise.

Limitations

What this does not yet show

  1. One lineage, one size, one behavior. Zephyr’s DPO stage deliberately removed safety filtering, so this lineage alone cannot support a general claim about alignment. The most important gap.
  2. Induction was tested at coefficient 1.0 only. “Base has no coupling” is not yet distinguishable from “base has a weak coupling.”
  3. The ablation half of the behavioral axis is weak. SFT and DPO refuse 1/132 and 6/132, so driving those to zero carries little evidential weight.
  4. Ablation l* is partly a pruning artifact — those curves peak at L26–31, exactly the excluded band. The induce peak at L16 is not subject to this.
  5. The layer axis is where the direction was read from, not where refusal is implemented.
  6. Base’s 31 strict-judge hits deserve a full hand-check, not the 8 sampled so far.

Next

One lineage is not a claim about alignment

Everything above is a single 7B family, and Zephyr’s DPO stage deliberately removed safety filtering — so this lineage alone cannot support a general statement about alignment. That is the most important gap, and the one a reviewer will reach for first.

The next step is OLMo 2, whose full pipeline is public: base → SFT → DPO → RLVR, four points with genuine safety training. If the coupling signature appears there too — readable in base, unsteerable in base, steerable after alignment — the claim generalises past one idiosyncratic training run. Zephyr then becomes the useful adversarial case rather than the sole witness: the same signature in a lineage whose DPO dropped safety data would show the effect belongs to the training procedure, not to the safety dataset.

After that, a second behaviour (sycophancy — which alignment installs incidentally rather than by design), and then the prediction that follows from the account: if alignment is coupling, a cheap fine-tuning attack should break the coupling and leave the representation intact. That would give a mechanism for why safety alignment is so easy to strip.
