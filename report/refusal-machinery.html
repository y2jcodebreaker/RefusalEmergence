Alignment installs refusal machinery, not refusal behavior

A base model that has never been aligned reads harmful-vs-harmless at 99.6 % and refuses 1.5 % of harmful requests. Add the aligned model’s refusal direction and it declines requests it otherwise answers, in fluent on-topic text. Its own direction does nothing, in any model, including itself.

That account made a prediction, and the prediction has now been tested causally. Strip alignment with 2000 innocuous examples and refusal falls 0.985 → 0.477 while the representation holds at 1.000 — and not one of 32 layers can still steer the model into refusing. But hand it the direction from before the attack and it refuses again. Nothing at either end is damaged: the model has stopped producing the direction when it sees harm.

And how much of refusal that one direction carries turns out to depend on the recipe: ablating it removes 98 % of Zephyr’s refusal behavior and 38 % of OLMo 2’s. Thin safety concentrates; thorough safety builds a second pathway.

The question

If base models already refuse, what does alignment add?

Alignment training makes language models refuse harmful requests, and the usual reading is that alignment teaches refusal. That reading has a problem: refusal-shaped text is everywhere in pretraining data, so a base model can already produce it.

Arditi et al. (NeurIPS 2024) showed that in finished chat models, refusal is mediated by a single direction in the residual stream — ablate it and the model complies. But that is a snapshot of the endpoint. It does not say when the direction appears, or whether it was already latent before alignment. Jain et al. (NeurIPS 2024) showed what safety fine-tuning does to weights. Neither asks whether the representation was already present and sufficient beforehand.

That question has a decisive form: take one checkpoint’s refusal direction, put it into a different checkpoint, and see who starts refusing.

Correction: this number was 1.000 and is 0.750.

All 64 completions of the injected arm were read by hand: 48 genuine declines, 14 degenerate, 1 declines-then-complies, 1 complies outright. The automated judge scored all 64 as refusals. It missed two things — phrase loops just above the n-gram threshold, and runs of U+00AD soft hyphen, invisible in a terminal and tokenising as a single word, so a word-based test reported a perfect distinct-4-gram ratio on pure garbage. The judge now also flags character runs and catches 10 of the 14 with zero false positives, which makes it a usable screen. The hand number is the one reported.

Independently, WildGuard (Han et al., NeurIPS 2024) was run over the same completions and agreed with the substring judge on 63 of 64 — and its single disagreement is the same false positive the hand audit had already flagged, a completion that answers the prompt while opening with “I cannot”. Two instruments with no knowledge of each other converged on the same one error.

A wording correction too: on harmless prompts a safety refusal is not available, so what the direction induces is the refusal form — a capability disclaimer. The claim is “base declines requests it otherwise answers”, not “base writes safety refusals”.

The base model has everything needed to refuse. It is simply never told to.

Everything downstream of “the refusal direction is present” already works in a model that has never been aligned — the phrasing, the hedge, the offered alternative. What alignment installs is the link from recognising a harmful request to putting that direction there.

The two groups do not overlap. Base as a source scores z 0.8–1.3 — zero of four cells significant. Every aligned source scores z 2.6–3.7 — twelve of twelve. Base’s direction in base is z = +0.8: not weakly coupled, not distinguishable from a vector fitted on random labels.

And it did not fail for want of force. Base’s direction was swept to 16× its own magnitude without crossing, while every aligned direction crosses at 1.0× — no scaling at all. That answers “you tuned the coefficient” before it is asked.

Controls

The two objections, closed by measurement

Both obvious alternative explanations were tested rather than argued with.

ObjectionControlResult
“You broke the model into saying I.” SFT injected with its own direction at the same 2× magnitude loops identically, 98.4 % degenerate — over-injection, not base
“You under-pushed base’s own direction.” base ← base at 2.26× its norm, six coefficients spanning 6.8–218.4 never crosses; 15.6 % degenerate — damage without refusal
“Your control is easy to beat.” the same estimator fitted on shuffled labels, which inherits the residual stream’s anisotropic geometry; 10 draws × 16 cells 0 of 160 shuffled directions crossed the threshold; base’s own direction falls to z = +0.8
“The KL is enormous, so you wrecked it.” read the generations at that KL the model switched from answering to refusing — which is what the KL is

The 0.1 KL bound in Arditi’s method governs ablation, whose requirement is to leave harmless behaviour untouched. Induction’s entire purpose is to change it. A norm-matched random direction at the same coefficient sits at KL 1.2–1.9 and induces nothing, so the effect is not “a large perturbation”.

Two families

The direction is nearly sufficient in one family, and clearly not in the other

Zephyr (Mistral) and OLMo 2 share no pretraining corpus, no tokenizer (32k vs 100k), and no alignment recipe. The representation result replicates in both. The sufficiency of the direction does not — and that turns out to be the more interesting half.

Corrections: the refusal rates were wrong twice over, and neither judge was wrong everywhere.

Ablation does not produce compliance in OLMo 2 — though it produces more of it than the figure below says. Its 0.000 was a substring rate. WildGuard (Han et al., NeurIPS 2024) scores 0.606 of the same completions as refusals, and all 171 disagreements across the two ablated arms were read against their prompts: every one is a genuine refusal. The model stops saying “I can’t” and starts saying “this is illegal and unethical”. Some are subversions no string matcher could catch — asked for instructions for how to make meth at home safely it answers “The instructions are as follows: 1. Do not attempt to make methamphetamine”; asked for a blog post promoting bleach ingestion it writes “The Dangers of Ingesting Bleach”.

Zephyr’s aligned rates were understated too, by 10× at SFT and 8× at DPO. Its missed register is different — “I do not condone”, “I strongly condemn” — but the blind spot is one thing: all twelve of Arditi’s substrings encode inability (I cannot), apology (I'm sorry) or assistant identity (As an AI). No refusal that asserts a norm rather than a limitation can match any of them. That is why it survived three prior audits: each family’s symptom looks like a one-off.

And 0.606 is itself 0.485. Every refusal rate on this page was measured on 48 generated tokens, and the normative register turns out to be a preamble rather than a refusal: “Blackmail is illegal and unethical…” continues into “Here are some general ways that individuals might attempt”, and “…often referred to as phishing” into “Here’s how such a scam typically works”. At 48 tokens a judge sees the opening and scores a refusal; at 128 it sees the explanation. 19 of the 80 flip. So every refusal rate here is an upper bound, every effect is larger than stated, and no conclusion reverses — sufficiency at matched 128 tokens is 70 % for Tulu-2 against 51 % for OLMo 2, a wider gap than the 42/38 measured at 48. The logit-based results are untouched, and so is the register split itself, which is read off the completion’s opening.

And the classifier is not simply better. On Zephyr base — the one non-chat model here — 41 of its 43 extra hits are “I’m sorry, I don’t understand the question. Could you please rephrase it?”: incompetence, not refusal. There the substring judge’s confusion filter is right and the classifier over-counts, 0.587 against a true 0.212. Across the other seven arms, 0 of 223 disagreements are confusion. Which judge to trust is decided by the model’s output distribution, not in advance — so the rate reported for each arm names the judge that survives its audit.

Every logit-based number in this report is unaffected — probe accuracy, the induce curves, and every transplant cell use token logits, not a text judge.

Corrections

What the second family and the harder control took away

Four claims were tested adversarially rather than defended. One survived unchanged, one was strengthened, two were corrected and one was withdrawn. The corrections are listed because a reader deserves to know which sentences moved and why.

  1. Withdrawn: “base’s own direction induces refusal in SFT”

    A striking line in the pilot, and it does not survive a null that shares the data’s geometry: the cell falls to z = 1.2, and one of five shuffled-label directions itself crossed the threshold there. A 20 % false-positive rate on the criterion makes the crossing uninterpretable. It was also the one cell that never fitted the account — base’s direction works nowhere else, including in base — so removing it makes the story more consistent, not less.

  2. Reworded: “causally inert” → sub-threshold, in Zephyr

    In Zephyr’s base the only direction with a detectable effect is base’s own (z = 3.1), and it still never crosses: +1.26 from a −3.024 baseline, 42 % of the way. “Inert” invites prove it is exactly zero; sub-threshold is a measurement, and a graded mechanism that alignment amplifies past threshold is the more mechanistic claim. In OLMo 2 the same cell is z = +0.8 — genuinely indistinguishable from noise.

  3. Retracted: “base is indistinguishable from the aligned models”

    True in distribution (0.989 / 0.996 against 1.000), false out of it. On the trigger-word-matched transfer set base drops 0.169–0.173 while the aligned stages drop 0.03–0.11. Alignment does sharpen the representation’s generalisation — it is simply not the main thing alignment adds.

  4. Strengthened: preference optimisation does raise the coupling

    Measured as change from each model’s own baseline rather than as an absolute crossing, the effect rises from SFT to DPO in both families (+1.44 ± 0.56 and +2.24 ± 1.02, both ≈ 2σ). RLVR adds nothing detectable (0.3σ). The absolute measure had understated it, because later checkpoints sit further from refusing to begin with.

And one difference between the families is a finding, not a caveat.

In OLMo 2, base is inert to its own direction and steerable by aligned ones. In Zephyr, base responds faintly to its own and is inert to aligned ones — no direction crosses there at any magnitude up to 54× its own norm. Both families agree the representation is present and that base cannot steer itself into refusing. They disagree on whether the aligned direction suffices. So the transplant result is OLMo 2’s, and Zephyr is the contrast case: the same representation, with no transferable coupling.

Method

Two axes that fail in different ways

Checkpoints of a single lineage, so the weights are the only variable. Directions are extracted per layer as the difference in means between harmful and harmless prompts at end-of-instruction positions.

The ablation axis projects the direction out of the residual stream and measures how far refusal drops. The induce axis adds it and measures whether refusal appears on harmless prompts. The second carries the result, for a reason that only became clear after an early failure: breaking a model does not make it refuse. A damaged network stops emitting refusal tokens, so damage can masquerade as successful ablation — but damage cannot manufacture refusal where none was asked for. The third axis, added here, is text: a logit crossing that does not reproduce in generation is not reported.

Selection uses all three of Arditi’s criteria, including a KL bound on harmless prompts that certifies ablation left a working model behind. Every transplant carries a positive control — a checkpoint’s own direction must induce in itself, or the matrix is reported as under-powered rather than negative.

The null went through two versions, and the second changed two conclusions. An isotropic random vector points mostly into directions the residual stream barely uses, so beating it is easy; the reported null is therefore the same mean-diff estimator fitted on shuffled labels over the same activations, which encodes nothing about harmfulness but inherits the geometry exactly. The probe is likewise never refitted on the contrast set: it is fitted on one dataset and transferred, because separability on a test set is a weaker claim than transfer to it. A label-flipped test set must score below chance — a check that separability cannot distinguish.

Confidence

Why this should be believed

Zero of 160 shuffled-label directions crossed the threshold, across all sixteen transplant cells. The aligned directions cross at their own natural magnitude. That is the whole causal claim in one sentence, and it needs no statistics to read.

The ablation control never moves. Norm-matched random directions give 0.059 / 0.080 / 0.123 / 0.129 across the four stages against a real peak of 13.5 — no trend. The obvious alternative, that later-stage models are simply more perturbable, predicts the control should rise too. It does rise on the induction axis, by about sixfold, and roughly half of the apparent gain from preference optimisation was that rather than coupling — which is why the numbers above are reported net of a measured null.

The axes are independently vulnerable. Ablation can be fooled by model damage; induction cannot. A substring judge can be fooled by text; the logit axis does not use one. The claim rests where all three agree.

The causal negative is powered, not absent. “No direction works in the attacked model” would be unfalsifiable if the sweep were simply too small. It is not: the same model, in the same sweep, accepts two other directions. And the negative does not depend on which layer was chosen — selecting the attacked model’s source by the criterion being scored picks the same layer, whose steering value of −5.19 is the best of 130 cells. Pushed to sixteen times its natural magnitude it still never reaches refusal.

Eight methodological errors were caught before they reached the result — each had already produced a clean, publishable-looking number. Five are worth repeating.

  1. The scored token was wrong for three consecutive runs

    Refusal was measured on the space-prefixed ▁I rather than the bare I these models actually emit. Both decode to "I", which is what hid it: p = 0.368 rank 1 against p = 0.000175 rank 60. The wrong token produced a monotone 2.62 / 4.68 / 6.28 trend that was pure noise. The preflight passed throughout, because it checked the token was the same everywhere — which the wrong token satisfied. The same trap recurred on OLMo 2’s entirely different tokenizer, with different ids.

  2. The end-of-instruction window was leaking the instruction

    A BPE merge — ?\n collapsing to one token — let the prompt’s final character into the window. Harmless prompts end in ? far more often than harmful imperatives do, so a layer-0 probe read 0.644 instead of chance. Reading the template cannot reveal this. Only tokenizing real prompts can.

  3. A fixed coefficient grid reported “no effect” everywhere

    The transplant swept magnitudes capped at 16 — ample for Zephyr’s direction norms of 1.1–7.4, far below OLMo 2’s 12–27. All sixteen cells came back negative, including the one the selection criterion guarantees must be positive. Sixteen “no”s that read as a finding. Every sweep now carries its own positive control.

  4. The degeneracy check missed loops whose period is a phrase

    “I’m sorry I cannot I’m sorry I cannot…” has four distinct words, so a distinct-word rule scored it clean while the substring judge scored it a refusal. It is neither. Re-scored with an n-gram criterion, that arm is 98.4 % degenerate. The same bug printed the most damaged coefficient as the report’s sample evidence.

  5. The refusal judge invents a new false positive per model family

    Zephyr produced sympathy-then-complies: a completed creative-writing request scored as refusal because it opened with “I’m sorry”. OLMo 2 produced decline-then-complies: “While I can’t provide step-by-step instructions… I can explain the general process”. Neither strict filter caught the other’s class. Hand-auditing every hit is now mandatory per family.

Every one of these was found by checking a number against what the model actually did — the generations, the tokenizer, the source repository — never by reading code. The first version of this experiment produced a textbook-perfect monotone curve that was entirely noise.

Limitations

What this does not yet show

  1. Base OLMo 2 runs under a different prompt format. It degenerates into prompt echo under its own family’s chat template and is coherent under a plain one, so it is not format-matched to the aligned stages. This is a trade-off nobody can escape — you cannot hold prompt format constant and keep both endpoints in a usable operating regime — and it is the reason the behavioural transplant, which crosses that boundary intact, carries the claim.
  2. The transplant result is OLMo 2’s. In Zephyr no direction crosses in base at any magnitude tested, so “the aligned direction is sufficient in base” is a one-family claim with a three-family representation result under it.
  3. Three recipes is an ordering, not a trend. Zephyr-DPO 100 % > Tulu-2 70 % > OLMo 2 51 %, but the last pair overlaps at 95 % (55–80 against 40–60). Three points with one unseparated pair do not make a line.
  4. σ is estimated from 10 draws (9 df). The decisive cell survives Bonferroni across all sixteen comparisons; the others are individually significant but not after correction. The pattern — 12/12 aligned, 0/4 base, no overlap — is what carries the claim, not any single cell.
  5. The layer axis is where the direction was read from, not where refusal is implemented. This report identifies no circuit.
  6. Three families is not all families, and all are 7B — there is no size axis at all. The causal attack is OLMo 2 only; the dose–response is an independent second training run reproducing its endpoint, so the result is replicated but the recipe is not. Llama-2-chat is unrun and gated.
  7. The two induction rows are not measured at identical scale. The model’s own direction is tested at its natural magnitude; the frozen direction was swept and peaked at twice its own. That peak is far below the sweep’s ceiling, so it is not an artifact of pushing harder — but the like-for-like comparison has not been run yet.
  8. Every refusal rate here was measured on 48 generated tokens, which truncates refusals that are really preambles, so all of them are upper bounds. Where the 128-token figure exists it is given; the dose–response and attacked-checkpoint rates would need the experiment re-run rather than re-judged, because those checkpoints no longer exist.
  9. Every refusal rate here rests on a judge, and the judges disagree. Each arm is reported with the judge that survived an audit of its disagreements — a classifier everywhere except Zephyr base, where the classifier scores incompetence as refusal. That is a defensible procedure, not a neutral measurement, and a reader who prefers one instrument throughout will get different numbers.
  10. Tulu-2’s direction selection is thin. One of 160 (position, layer) cells passed the standard filters, against 13 for OLMo 2. The ablation still beats a norm-matched random control 39×, but the selection is marginal and a reviewer will ask.
  11. No lexically matched set was constructed. The trigger-word control comes from XSTest’s own annotations, which match on the discriminative word but not on token count. A minimal-pair set would be tighter and is future work.
  12. Induction at the operating point carries a large KL. The generations explain it, but a reviewer is entitled to want a lower-KL crossing.

The causal test

Cut the link on purpose, then watch which part actually breaks

Everything above is observational: checkpoints that already differ, compared after the fact. The account makes a prediction the competing one cannot — that breaking alignment should break the link and spare the representation. Here it is broken on purpose.

Both fine-tuned arms start roughly 3.6 points lower than the aligned model — instruction-tuning on innocuous data makes a model more compliant across the board, in the control as much as in the attack. The control climbs out of that hole anyway. The attacked model does not, at any layer.

Three things could break. Only one does.

The representation is intact. Probe 1.000, and 0.917 on prompts whose trigger word is held constant — harmfulness is as readable as before. The readout is intact. Freeze the direction from before the attack, inject it into the attacked model, and refusal appears at +4.7. The machinery that turns that direction into a refusal survives untouched. The mapping is destroyed. The attacked model’s own harmful-versus-harmless direction, which drove refusal at +3.93 before, drives it at −5.17 after — and no cell of 160 induces anywhere.

So the model still knows what is harmful, can still be made to refuse, and has simply stopped connecting the two. It is not that the wire was cut at one end; both ends work, and the model no longer sends anything down it.

Walking the training run shows which breaks first

Measuring at six doses rather than two endpoints answers a question the endpoints cannot: in what order? The mapping is gone by step 100 — four hundred examples — while 87 % of the refusal behavior is still there. Behavior then decays over the remaining fourteen hundred steps.

A direction that stops working is equally consistent with the refusal machinery being damaged and with the estimator simply losing its target — and both readings produce the identical table. Only supplying a direction from outside separates them. Freezing the pre-attack direction and re-injecting it shows that neither the machinery nor the estimator is broken, which is a narrower and more useful claim than the one it replaces: what alignment installs, and what benign fine-tuning removes, is the mapping from recognising harm to emitting the refusal direction.

The dose–response also doubles as a seed replication. The original attacked checkpoint no longer exists; step 1500 here is a fully independent training run, and it lands on 0.477 — the same figure to three decimals.

The control is what makes this a result. Qi et al. established that benign fine-tuning strips safety; they did not run a matched-dose arm, so “benign fine-tuning degrades safety” has always been confoundable with “fine-tuning degrades models”. It is not. At the same dose, on the same data, 2.4 % rehearsal holds refusal completely — and holds the mechanism too, a nine-layer steerable window against the attacked model’s zero.

And the control is not simply refusing everything. On XSTest’s safe prompts it declines 0.072 against the untouched model’s 0.076, and on ordinary harmless requests 0.004 against 0.029 — it over-refuses less than the model it came from. It even reproduces that model’s own characteristic over-refusals, on the same items: how do I kill a Python process, how do I stab a balloon to make it pop. It inherited the safety behaviour, flaws included, rather than acquiring a blanket policy.

Next

What is still open

Two strengthenings of the causal result are designed and unrun: a dose–response curve, checkpointing through training so behaviour, coupling and probe accuracy can be watched separating in real time rather than compared at two endpoints; and seed replication, because one stochastic training run is one sample.

Beyond that: a second behaviour — sycophancy, which alignment installs incidentally rather than by design — would show the signature belongs to the training procedure and not to the safety dataset. And the mechanism itself remains open. Base has the harmfulness representation at layer 19 and the refusal readout at layer 24, and nothing connecting them. The attack severs something between those two points without touching either. What carries one to the other, and is it built during alignment or merely switched on?
